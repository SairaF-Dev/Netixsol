# SQL Joins & Relational Database Analysis: DVD Rental Database

## Project Overview
This project explores relational database structures and demonstrates how to combine data from multiple related tables using SQL JOIN operations. Using the DVD Rental sample database, we extracted meaningful business insights by navigating complex relationships between customers, films, rentals, and payments.

## Database Structure & ER Diagram
The database is highly normalized. Please refer to the attached `screenshots/Diagram/ER_diagram.png` for the complete Entity-Relationship (ER) diagram detailing the schema.


### Key Relationships Used
To answer the business questions, I navigated the database schema using these primary relationship paths:

* **Customer Geography:** `customer` ➔ `address` ➔ `city` ➔ `country`
* **Transactions & Revenue:** `customer` ➔ `rental` ➔ `payment` | `rental` ➔ `inventory` ➔ `film`
* **Film Attributes (Genres):** `film` ➔ `film_category` ➔ `category`
* **Film Cast (Actors):** `film` ➔ `film_actor` ➔ `actor`

---

## SQL JOIN Explanations & Methodology
This project heavily utilizes `INNER JOIN` and `LEFT JOIN` to answer business questions. 

* **INNER JOIN:** Used when we only wanted records that existed in both tables. For example, when finding the film title for a payment, we only care about payments that have a matching rental and inventory record.
* **LEFT JOIN:** Used when we wanted to keep all records from the "left" table, even if there was no match in the "right" table. In Question 7, `LEFT JOIN` was used to count films per category, ensuring that a category with 0 films would still appear in the results (unlike an `INNER JOIN`, which would drop empty categories).

### How Business Questions Were Solved

**1. Display Customer Name, Email, City, and Country**
To retrieve the complete geographical details for each customer, I used a sequence of three `INNER JOIN` operations to connect the normalized tables: `customer` ➔ `address` ➔ `city` ➔ `country`. Additionally, I used the concatenation operator (`||`) to combine the first and last names into a single `customer_name` column for better readability. *(Ref: `part2_01_customer_info.png`)*

**2. Display every payment with Customer Name, Film Title, and Amount Paid**
To link the financial transaction to the specific rented product, I connected the `payment` table to the `film` table by traversing the relationship path: `payment` ➔ `rental` ➔ `inventory` ➔ `film` using `INNER JOIN`s. I also joined the `customer` table directly to `payment` to retrieve the customer's name, formatting it into a single column using the `CONCAT()` function. *(Ref: `part2_02_payment_customer_film.png` & `part2_03_payment_customer_film.png`)*

**3. Find the Top 10 customers based on total amount spent**
To identify the highest-spending customers, I used an `INNER JOIN` to connect the `customer` and `payment` tables via `customer_id`. I then used the `SUM()` aggregate function on the payment amounts, grouped the results by each customer, and sorted the total spend in descending order using `ORDER BY ... DESC`. Finally, I applied a `LIMIT 10` clause to restrict the output to only the top 10 records. *(Ref: `part2_04_top10_customer_spent_total_amount.png`)*

**4. Display each film with its Category and Rental Rate**
To associate films with their respective genres, I resolved the many-to-many relationship between the `film` and `category` tables by using `INNER JOIN`s through the associative junction table, `film_category`. The query retrieves the film title, the category name, and the rental rate, finally organizing the output alphabetically by the film's title using `ORDER BY`. *(Ref: `part2_05_film_category_rental_rate.png`)*

**5. Find all actors who appeared in each film**
To link films with their cast members, I resolved the many-to-many relationship between the `film` and `actor` tables by utilizing `INNER JOIN`s through the `film_actor` junction table. I used the `CONCAT()` function to combine the actors' first and last names into a single `actor_name` column, and applied an `ORDER BY` clause to sort the results alphabetically by the film's title, and then by the actor's name. *(Ref: `part2_06_actors_in_each_film.png`)*

**6. Count how many films belong to each category**
To determine the volume of films within each genre, I used a `LEFT JOIN` to connect the `category` table to the `film_category` table. Using a `LEFT JOIN` ensures that every category is included in the final report, even if it currently has zero films associated with it. I then applied the `COUNT()` aggregate function to the film IDs, grouped the results by the category name, and sorted the output in descending order to show the most populated categories at the top. *(Ref: `part2_07_film_count_per_category.png`)*

**7. Which categories generated the highest revenue?**
To determine the total revenue generated by each film genre, I constructed a multi-table query that links financial transactions all the way to film categories. Using `INNER JOIN`s, I connected the `payment` table through a sequence of intermediate tables: `rental` ➔ `inventory` ➔ `film` ➔ `film_category` ➔ `category`. I then applied the `SUM()` aggregate function to the payment amounts, grouped the results by the category name, and sorted the final output in descending order to highlight the most profitable genres. *(Ref: `part2_08_highest_revenue_categories.png`)*

**8. Find customers who have rented more than 20 films**
To identify high-volume renters, I connected the `customer` and `rental` tables using an `INNER JOIN` via the `customer_id`. I used the `COUNT()` aggregate function to calculate the total number of rentals per customer and grouped the results by the customer's details. Crucially, I applied a `HAVING` clause to filter the aggregated data, restricting the final output to only those customers with a rental count strictly greater than 20. I also used `CONCAT()` to format the names into a single column. *(Ref: `part2_09_customers_rented_more_than_20.png`)*

**9. Which cities generated the highest rental revenue?**
To determine the revenue generated by each location, I linked the geographical tables to the financial transactions using a chain of `INNER JOIN`s: `city` ➔ `country` ➔ `address` ➔ `customer` ➔ `payment`. I applied the `SUM()` aggregate function to the payment amounts to calculate the total revenue, grouped the data by both city and country, and then sorted the final results in descending order using `ORDER BY` to highlight the most profitable cities. *(Ref: `part2_10_highest_revenue_cities.png`)*

**10. Bonus Challenge: Which actor has generated the highest total rental revenue?**
Because there is no direct relationship between actors and payments, I had to determine the shortest path through the database schema to connect them. I used a sequence of five `INNER JOIN` operations traversing the path: `actor` ➔ `film_actor` ➔ `film` ➔ `inventory` ➔ `rental` ➔ `payment`. I applied the `SUM()` aggregate function to the payment amounts, grouped the data by the actor's details, and sorted the total revenue in descending order using `ORDER BY` to reveal the highest-grossing actors at the top. I also used `CONCAT()` to merge the first and last names into a single `actor_name` column. *(Ref: `part2_bonus_highest_revenue_actor.png`)*

## Business Insights

Based on the query results, here are three actionable business insights for the DVD Rental store:

1. **Inventory vs. Demand Alignment:** The `Sports` category is both the most stocked genre (74 films) and the highest revenue generator ($4,892.19), showing excellent alignment with customer demand. However, `Sci-Fi` is the second-highest revenue generator ($4,336.01) despite not being in the top genres by inventory count. **Action:** The business should acquire more Sci-Fi titles to capture this unmet demand and boost sales.
2. **VIP Customer Retention:** There is a clear demographic of "power users" driving significant revenue, such as Eleanor Hunt (top spender at $211.55) and Priscilla Lowe (highest volume with 35 rentals). **Action:** Implement a VIP loyalty or rewards program specifically targeting customers who rent more than 20 films to ensure the retention of these high-value individuals.
3. **Star Power Drives Sales:** Specific actors bring in massively disproportionate revenue. For example, films featuring Gina Degeneres generated $3,129.17, which is nearly $600 more than the second-highest grossing actor. **Action:** Marketing campaigns and the store's "Featured" section should prominently highlight movies starring these top-grossing actors to maximize rental conversion rates.
